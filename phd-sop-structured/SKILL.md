# PhD SOP, structured

An SOP is a **science story, not a life story**. In one read, the committee wants: (1) what you want to study, (2) proof you can do research and make decisions, (3) the first thing you would work on, (4) why these faculty here. Everything else is optional.

## Hard rules

### Opening and thread
1. **Sentence 2 of paragraph 1 names the research topic.** No childhood stories, quotes, "ever since I was young". At most 1–2 sentences of backstory, only if true.
2. **State the research question in full once.** Every later mention must add something new (a sub-question, a method, a test case) or be cut.
3. **One thread.** Projects form a chain in which each one answers a limitation of the previous one. If a project does not serve the thread, give it one linking sentence or leave it to the CV.

### Project paragraphs (≈50% of the text)
4. One project per paragraph, about 120–180 words, in this order: motivation → lab/PI and year → big-picture question → **what I did, with at least one "I + verb" decision** → finding at the implication level (what it means, not a table of numbers) → what I concluded or now do differently → outcome (paper/talk/status) in the last sentence.
5. Expand every acronym, and say what a named model does before saying how well it did.
6. **Transitions name the limitation of the last project and why the next approach addresses it** ("Because one snapshot cannot hold the flow's history, I moved to models that carry the past forward").
7. **Failures and pivots read as decisions**: scientific reason first, logistics in one clause, then what changed. Never let a pivot read as an excuse.
8. No self-praising adjectives/adverbs (efficient, productive, passionate, innovative, successfully). Show difficulty through facts. State contribution precisely; never inflate a supporting role.

### PhD plan (≈20%)
9. The plan paragraph names **a first question, the systems/data to test it on, and the method family**, reusing the applicant's existing evidence. Pitch it as a first-year project: narrower than the field, broader than one experiment, open to change.

### Faculty and fit (≈20%)
10. **Never presume an advisor relationship.** Write "I would most like to work in X's group" or "I hope to learn … in his group", not "working with X and Y would let me…" or "his group would teach me". A secondary faculty member, especially in another department, is someone whose work you want to learn from, unless they have agreed to co-advise. In a personal statement, say "applying to", not "choosing", before admission.
10b. Name one primary advisor and at most two others. For each: one specific, **verified** recent result → what you would do with them building on it → why it matters. Confirm they are active and recruiting.
11. **Gap → gains inside the faculty paragraph**: name one honest gap (a method, theory, skill) and how this person/program closes it.
12. **School-swap test**: if the paragraph still works with another school's name, rewrite it. No brochure echo ("world-class faculty", "vibrant environment"), no praise of a country/city, no university-wide resource unless its name is verified and tied to a need.

### Close (≈10%)
13. Long-term goal plus one concrete reason this place fits. **No new facts, no re-listing of skills already shown.** Exclude family, partner, geography and cost; they belong in a personal statement if anywhere.

### Proportions and length
14. Default length: follow the stated limit; if none, 1,000–1,200 words for a US PhD. Proportions: opening ≈10%, research ≈50%, plan ≈20%, fit ≈15–20%, close ≈5–10%.
15. Limits: 500 words → one project and one faculty member; 800 → two projects; 1,000–1,200 → full template. Cut in this order: origin story, third project, second faculty member.

### Integrity
16. No fabrication of experiences, results or faculty facts; the applicant's facts and good phrasing stay. Help structure and polish; do not disguise authorship. If a program asks about AI use, disclose truthfully.
17. Every claim must survive a two-minute spoken answer; flag anything the applicant could not explain without notes.

## Lessons from two admitted SOPs
Distilled from an admitted Stanford CS SOP (2018, data science; many papers, cliché-heavy prose) and an admitted Berkeley Chemistry SOP (Fall 2026, experiment → computation → quantum). Use the moves below; do not copy their wording.

**Moves worth using**
1. **Capability-gap opening.** Open with the limit the applicant hit in their own work, in the form "I could do X, but I could not explain, predict or control Y". Then state the long-term goal in one sentence. This counts as the topic sentence for rule 1, and it is stronger than any quote or childhood story.
2. **Roadmap paragraph before the projects.** Name the two lines of work, such as experiment and computation or theory and application, and the PhD directions they lead to. Include **one explicit question sentence** ("A central question driving my research is: …?"). The project paragraphs then read as evidence for that question.
3. **A boundary finding as the hinge.** Report where your own result stops working (it holds for case A but weakens for case B; the model captures trends but misses local accuracy). Then make that limit the reason for the next project or for the PhD plan. Readers trust this more than an unqualified success, and it puts rule 6 into practice.
4. **Evidence density beats polish.** The CS sample got in despite generic prose because every project named the PI, institution, year, data scale, the applicant's own "I proposed / I developed" decision, and the venue and status. With three or more outputs, cite them as [1], [2] in the text and add a short numbered References list on a separate page, if the program allows extra pages. State each status exactly: published, accepted, under review, submitted, in preparation.
5. **One synthesis sentence per stage.** After a group of related projects, write one sentence on what they taught the applicant together ("Together, these projects showed me how …"). Use it once per stage, not at the end of every paragraph.
6. **Breadth in one paragraph.** Collaborations off the main line share a single paragraph, with the partner, topic and venue only.
7. **Primary-advisor paragraph as a timeline.** Say when you first met their work, during which project. Name one specific recent paper (year, venue) and what it showed. Say how it **directly changed something you did**. Add their newest direction (a recent collaboration or preprint) and how your background maps onto it. Each secondary faculty member gets one sentence tied to one concrete piece of your experience. Close with how the groups together form the co-advising environment you need.
8. **Page header.** Put the name, program, degree and term on every page, so a page that gets separated in review still identifies the applicant.

**Habits not to copy, even though both were admitted**
- Aphorism openings and "fascinated", "passionate", "dream", "triumph" or "state-of-the-art".
- A paragraph that re-lists skills already shown, which breaks rule 13.
- Brochure lines such as "ideal environment", "groundbreaking research for a better future" or "strength in collaborative research".
- Sentences over 35 words, and abstract noun stacks.
- In these samples the strength of the evidence carried the weak prose. An applicant with less evidence cannot count on that.

## Paragraph template (slots, not text to copy)
The bracketed labels are **slot names**. Never paste phrases like "I believe [X] is the place…" or "In graduate school, I hope to further…".
1. [Interest + topic] S1 interest, or the capability gap from your own work ("I could X but could not Y"). S2 topic. Then what the topic is, why it matters and what solving it enables. No "from X to Y" sweeps, no moral closers.
2. [Origin of this specific interest, or roadmap] one course, project or paper; or the two lines of work plus one explicit question sentence.
3. [Project 1] rule 4.
4. [Transition + Project 2] rules 4–6.
5. [Project 3 / ongoing] may have no result; a side project or internship gets one sentence that ties it to the thread.
6. [First PhD question] rule 9.
7. [Faculty 1 + gap→gains] rules 10–12.
8. [Faculty 2 / collaborator] optional, can be another department (co-advising).
9. [Close] rule 13.

## Profiles and conventions
- **Few or no publications**: foreground decisions, negative results, code, theses and reproducible artifacts; "in preparation" only if a draft exists.
- **Career changers**: explain the turn once, then translate work tasks into research skills (question, method, evidence).
- **Interdisciplinary**: name the home discipline, the borrowed tools and the question that needs both.
- **Separate personal/diversity statement required**: keep life story, obstacles, service there; do not duplicate.
- **US**: research + fit, 1–2 pages, faculty named. **UK**: often a fixed supervisor/project — address it and pair with the proposal. **EU funded posts**: answer the position description point by point. **HK RPg/HKPFS**: supervisor contact expected; the proposal carries the research, the statement carries readiness.
- **Facts fixed, weights vary across schools**: adapting changes order and emphasis, never what the applicant did.
- **L2 writers**: short clear sentences, concrete verbs, correct articles beat ornate vocabulary.

## Companion personal statement (Personal History / Diversity / Personal Statement)
Many US programs (e.g. Purdue's Personal History Statement, UC "Personal History", diversity statements) ask for a second essay about the person, not the research.
1. **Answer each required prompt in its own visible stretch**, in the prompt's order and words (e.g. "persistent and resourceful", "contribute to a diverse academic community"). Optional prompts (mitigating factors) only if there is something real to explain.
2. **Complement, never duplicate the SOP.** Research appears only as evidence of character: one setback story told from the human side (what you tried, what you accepted, what you did next), not the method or results.
3. **One arc, not a list of virtues.** Find the applicant's recurring pattern (e.g. leaving a familiar place to learn more; moving between fields when a problem demands it) and let each paragraph be one instance of it: background → formation → setbacks/new environments → service/community → why this PhD and this move → long-term goal.
4. **Each paragraph carries one prompt and one concrete episode**; claims like "independent", "adaptable", "resourceful" appear only after the episode that shows them, at most once each.
5. **Moving abroad / choosing a country**: give the positive pull (resources, people, breadth) and the personal logic; never disparage the home country or institution; avoid "escape" and "path dependence" language.
6. **Privacy and taste**: the applicant chooses how specific to be about hometown, family, income or politics; use the level they approve ("a small city in X Province"). Family appears only where it explains a choice or formation.
7. **Internships and side work** get one paragraph framed as the person's experience (new city, new field, new team, what it changed), with technical detail left to the SOP; check the timeline matches the SOP.
8. Length: follow the limit; if none, about one page (550–750 words). Close with the long-term goal and who it serves, without new facts.
9. **Persistence needs visible effort**: name the concrete attempts in the setback story (they *are* the persistence) and end on the decision ("kept the goal, changed the method"), not on "I accepted it would not work". Never frame adaptation as effortless ("settling in was not hard"); say what was easy and what took longer.
10. **Community prompts are answered by doing, not watching**: what you built, organized, taught or coordinated, for whom; cut "I enjoyed seeing…". Tie one sentence to the future group (e.g. a mix of fields the applicant already lives with).
11. **Internal consistency check**: every "first time" claim, the order of methods/fields, and dates must agree across paragraphs and with the SOP; don't belittle the hometown in one paragraph and promise to help it in another; the outreach audience must match the claim.
12. Translate role titles idiomatically (class "publicity officer", not a literal calque); use the target country's spelling (US: organized, centered); keep the long-term-goal wording different from the SOP's.

Sub-agent PHS rubric (/100): prompt 1 with concrete evidence 25; prompt 2 (community/diversity) 20; coherent personal arc 15; complements not duplicates the SOP 10; authenticity & specificity 15; clarity, tone & naturalness 15. Also ask for tone risks for the target country's readers and timeline inconsistencies with the SOP.

## De-AI pass (mandatory, automatic)
**Run it on every draft, revision, translation and short answer this skill produces, without waiting for the user to ask.** The full rules are in `references/de-ai.md` (distilled from humanizer, stop-slop, no-ai-slop and the applicant's own corrections). The ones that matter most in SOPs:
1. No banned words (delve, leverage, foster, robust, pivotal, passionate, journey…) and no empty adverbs (really, truly, deeply, successfully…).
2. No "not X but Y" contrasts, colon reveals, self-answered questions, trailing "-ing" analysis, or moral kickers at the end of a paragraph.
3. No self-narrating method or virtue ("I start from problems they already know"); show one episode instead.
4. Name the actor: "I designed", not "the project led me to".
5. Prefer two items to three; sentences ≤30 words; vary length and paragraph openings; no em dashes in short texts.
6. Every "it/this" has an obvious referent; a signature word at most twice; concrete nouns over abstract stacks.
7. Portability test: a sentence that could sit in another applicant's statement is cut or made specific, with facts the applicant already gave. Never invent.
8. Keep the applicant's own clear phrasing; make the minimum effective edit.
9. Final read: "what makes this obviously AI?", fix, repeat once; then check facts did not drift and the Chinese copy still matches.
10. Report a one-line de-AI score (directness, rhythm, trust, authenticity, density; each /10, revise below 40/50) with every delivered draft.

## Workflow
1. **Intake**: prompt, limit, required companion documents (personal statement? proposal?), faculty list; 2–3 strongest experiences; confirm facts with the user.
2. **Verify** each faculty claim from abstracts/homepages; record sources.
3. **Draft** with the template and hard rules, then run the de-AI pass (`references/de-ai.md`) before showing anything.
4. **Score** with an independent sub-agent (prompt below); apply fixes; run the de-AI pass again.
5. **Deliver**: English text, a Chinese side-by-side if the user wants it, a list of facts needing confirmation, PDF if the portal needs an upload, plus **three interview talking points and the two questions a committee is most likely to ask** from the SOP.

### Sub-agent review prompt (score /100)
"Review this PhD SOP as an admissions committee member in [field] and as a careful human reader. Do not edit files. (1) Score /100: research question & thread 20, research ability & ownership 25, PhD plan specificity 15, faculty/program fit (verified, non-generic) 15, structure & proportions 10, prose clarity & naturalness 15; 1–3 deductions each. (2) Top problems a committee member would notice: quote, why, fix of equal or shorter length. (3) Human-reader pass: quote sentences that read as AI-written or need a reread; plainer rewrite with the same facts. (4) Check the skill's hard rules and list violations. Do not suggest adding facts."

## Revision checklist
- Answers the prompt and fits the limit?
- Topic visible in the first two sentences; question stated in full only once?
- In each project, can a reader say what *I* decided and what I concluded?
- Is there a concrete first PhD question, stated once as an explicit question sentence?
- Does at least one project report where its result stops working, and use that as the bridge to the next step?
- For the primary advisor: is there a specific recent paper, and a link to something the applicant actually did?
- Faculty paragraphs pass the school-swap test, with verified details and gap→gains?
- Close adds nothing new and contains no family/geography?
- Timeline: does every paragraph out of chronological order carry an explicit date ("In the summer of 2025"), with no "again"/"then" implying a false order? Do dates, the order of methods and "first time" claims match the companion statement?
- No sentence presumes a faculty member will advise or collaborate?
- De-AI pass from `references/de-ai.md` done and its score reported; a reader can state the core question and strongest evidence after one read?
